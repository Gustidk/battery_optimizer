How the grid’s response is modelled

Solar and wind production

Every generator delivers its installed power times a fraction between 0 and 1 that follows the weather hour by hour. Change the formulas’ values here and the next run uses them.

Solar (PV)
fraction = min( 1 , PR × GHI ÷ Gfull )
power = installed MW × fraction

GHI is the global horizontal irradiance from the weather data. PR collects losses from temperature, inverter and dirt (100 % = none).

Wind turbines (wind speed at 100 m)
fraction = 0  below vin and above vout
fraction = ((v − vin) ÷ (vrated − vin))k  from vin to vrated
fraction = 1  from vrated to vout

Battery size sensitivity

Runs the chosen placement for every combination of battery power and energy up to the limits below and shows how much the weighted squared peaks fall. It uses the piecewise AC model, so the sizes are compared fairly, and the grid response is measured once and shared between sizes.

Adds a second plan for the same battery: charge when electricity is cheap and discharge when it is expensive. The results then show where this plan differs from the grid-friendly plan and what grid-friendliness costs in revenue. Spot price only: no tariffs or taxes.

Method

From a network to a grid-friendly schedule

The planner answers one question: if a battery sits at a given bus, when should it charge and discharge so that the lines and transformers of the whole network carry as little peak load as possible? It follows four steps, and every number shown under Results comes from step 4.

1. Baseline year

Every hour gets a full AC load flow (Newton-Raphson) without a battery. Loads follow their daily profile; PV follows irradiance relative to 1000 W/m²; wind turbines follow a generic cubic power curve (cut-in 3 m/s, rated 12 m/s, cut-out 25 m/s).

2. How the grid responds to the battery

The planner measures, with AC load flows, how each element’s loading changes when the battery injects or absorbs power. Three options trade speed for accuracy: one sensitivity per element at the busiest hour, a sensitivity for every hour, or the real loading at several power levels each hour (a convex, piecewise linear model).

3. Optimise the schedule

Objective 1 minimise (1/n) Σₗ peakₗ Objective 3 minimise Σₗ wₗ · peakₗ² subject to peakₗ ≥ loadingₗ(t, battery power at t) for every element l and hour t soc[t] = soc[t−1] + ηc·charge[t] − discharge[t]/ηd 0 ≤ charge, discharge ≤ P, 0 ≤ soc ≤ E, soc at the end = soc at the start

Objective 1 is a linear programme solved with HiGHS; objective 3 and the piecewise model are solved with Clarabel. The piecewise model is solved twice: the second pass locks each hour to charging, discharging or idle, so the battery never charges and discharges at the same time.

4. Validate

The schedule is run through a new AC time series with the battery in place. Loadings, voltages and the exchange with the transmission grid are reported from this run, not from the optimisation model.

Assumptions

  • One battery at a time, unity power factor, hourly steps.
  • Fixed topology and transformer taps over the period. Loads are constant power.
  • Transformer vector groups (for example Dyn11) enter as their phase shift, clock number × 30°. In a balanced load flow this only matters for transformers in parallel or in a ring, and those must share the same clock number. Winding type and neutral grounding affect the zero-sequence system, which is not modelled.
  • Supported elements: buses, external grids, lines, two-winding transformers, line switches, shunts, loads and static generators (PV and wind).
  • Thermal loading is current relative to the line’s max_i_ka or the transformer’s rated current, as in pandapower.

Accuracy

The load flow reproduces pandapower and lightsim2grid to within 10⁻⁶ percentage points on the CIGRE MV network and on 40 randomly generated test networks. On CIGRE MV it reproduces the optimised peak loadings of the original Python study for all three models and both objectives.

Runs on your computer

The engine is Python (numpy, scipy, HiGHS, Clarabel) compiled to WebAssembly with Pyodide, and it runs in your browser. Nothing you enter leaves your device. The first visit downloads about 31 MB, which the browser then keeps.
